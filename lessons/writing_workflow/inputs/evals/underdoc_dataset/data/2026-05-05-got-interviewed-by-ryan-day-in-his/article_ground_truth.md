# Got interviewed by Ryan Day in his Tip Sheet issue #65 and some extra thoughts

Ryan Day (author of _Hands-On APIs for AI and Data Science,_ O’Reilly) and writer of the Tip Sheet newsletter featured me in his [issue #65](https://ryan-day.kit.com/posts/tip-sheet-65-data-scientist-doctor-football-aficionado) last week.

He asked eight questions about my background, my stack, and how I learn.

There were three of them I wanted to expand a bit and share some thoughts with you.

* * *

### Q4: Why are side projects valuable for people learning data science?

In a side project you set the tone of what you want to build, why it matters, and how to measure if it worked.

You pick a problem you care enough about, you define the requirements, the scope, the technology and the dataset. It forces you to learn and think with a kind of clarity that structured learning rarely does.

I started with football analytics because I wanted to apply what I was learning to something I cared about and found fun enough and it has led me to push my knowledge forwards and collaborate with special people I wouldn’t think I’d meet if I didn’t build these kind of projects.

Fun fact is: this also helped to me work on healthcare data/AI projects on my day job.

### Q6: What methods of skill development have you benefited from most?

I told Ryan I combine three: structured learning, project-based learning, and unstructured learning.

In this case my opinion is that the order matters.

Structured learning gives you the map and foundations to get the knowledge that you can then apply to projects. It doesn’t mean you need to learn everything there is about a topic and fall into rabbit holes, but just learning the necessary info to start building and leaving room for experimentation while you research stuff when you’re stuck.

On the other side unstructured learning like newsletters, blog posts, a well-curated social media timeline, keeps you updated to where the field is actually going. It can even give you some ideas to implement them yourself on your projects.

A dangerous trap is staying too long in one mode.

Entering tutorial hell by finishing a course and starting another course with no meaningful output (been there) creates an illusion of learning. Or jumping straight to building without a minimal foundation, spending weeks debugging something a couple hours read (or documentation) would have clarified.

The sweet spot is knowing when to shift from one to another. For example, if you’re new to something or you’re a bit confused, go with structured.

When you feel you got a good initial grasp of that knowledge, go build something even if it’s small, to make it stick.

If you feel you want to see how the field evolves, go with unstructured.

Right now I’m running all three in parallel: taking Agentic AI Engineering course at Towards AI Academy by Paul Iusztin and Louis-François Bouchard, applying it directly to a tool I’m building for myself, and constantly reading newsletters like Ryan’s to stay sharp on what people are shipping.

* * *

### Q7: What would you tell people who want to keep developing their technical skills?

To make it stick, I’ll put it inside quotes:

> Write about what you learn and share it online.

Not for personal branding but because explaining something forces you to actually understand it.

Every post I’ve written about RAG pipelines, MCP, or football data sources has exposed gaps in my own thinking that I didn’t know were there until I tried to put them into words. This makes me want to understand things better.

Th big myth we need to debunk is the need for a big audience. You actually don’t. The “trick” is to be consistent even if you have a “small audience” or you think nobody’s going to listen. There are people out there that will definitely benefit from your point of view.

You can write a bug you fixed, the trade-off you made when deciding on your tech stack, the framework you just learn from one field and how you applied it to solve a problem in another.

Maybe ten people will read it but one of them might be building the exact same thing and needed exactly that post. Or one of them might be writing a newsletter and looking for someone to interview 🤷🏻‍♂️

Your job is to make it exist.
